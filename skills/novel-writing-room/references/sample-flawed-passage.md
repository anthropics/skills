## The passage

Now, it is a fact, that there was nothing at all particular about the knocker on the door, except
that it was very large. It is also a fact, that Scrooge had seen it, night and morning, during his
whole residence in that place; also that Scrooge had as little of what is called fancy about him as
any man in the city of London, even including - which is a bold word - the corporation, aldermen, and
livery. Let it also be borne in mind that Scrooge had not bestowed one thought on Marley, since his
last mention of his seven years' dead partner that afternoon. And then let any man explain to me, if
he can, how it happened that Scrooge, having his key in the lock of the door, saw in the knocker,
without its undergoing any intermediate process of change - not a knocker, but Marley's face.

Marley's face. It was not in impenetrable shadow as the other objects in the yard were, but had a
dismal light about it, like a bad lobster in a dark cellar. It was not angry or ferocious, but looked
at Scrooge as Scrooge used to look: with ghostly spectacles turned up on its ghostly forehead. The
hair was curiously stirred, as if by breath or hot air; and, though the eyes were wide open, they
were perfectly motionless. That, and its livid colour, made it horrible; but its horror seemed to be
in spite of the face and beyond its control, rather than a part of its own expression.

As Scrooge looked fixedly at this phenomenon, it was a knocker again.

To say that he was not startled, or that his blood was not conscious of a terrible sensation to which
it had been a stranger from infancy, would be untrue. But he put his hand upon the key he had
relinquished, turned it sturdily, walked in, and lighted his candle.

He did pause, with a moment's irresolution, before he shut the door; and he did look cautiously
behind it first, as if he half expected to be terrified with the sight of Marley's pigtail sticking
out into the hall. But there was nothing on the back of the door, except the screws and nuts that
held the knocker on, so he said "Pooh, pooh!" and closed it with a bang. He was badly frightened,
and it showed how deeply the sight had shaken him.

The sound resounded through the house like thunder. Every room above, and every cask in the
wine-merchant's cellars below, appeared to have a separate peal of echoes of its own. Scrooge was
not a man to be frightened by echoes. He fastened the door, and walked across the hall, and up the
stairs; slowly too: lighting his candle as he went.

Up Scrooge went, not caring a button for that. Darkness is cheap, and Scrooge liked it. It was
Marley's ghost, of course, that he would find waiting for him above. But before he shut his heavy
door, he walked through his rooms to see that all was right. Walking through them, the face still
hung before his eyes.

Sitting-room, bedroom, lumber-room. All as they should be, but for a letter lying unopened upon the
table. Nobody under the table, nobody under the sofa; a small fire in the grate; spoon and basin
ready; and the little saucepan of gruel (Scrooge had a cold in his head) upon the hob. Nobody under
the bed; nobody in the closet; nobody in his dressing-gown, which was hanging up in a suspicious
attitude against the wall. Lumber-room as usual. Old fire-guard, old shoes, two fish-baskets,
washing-stand on three legs, and a poker.

Quite satisfied, he closed his door, and locked himself in; double-locked himself in, which was not
his custom. Scrooge smiled warmly at the thought of the face, and was glad of it. Thus secured
against surprise, he took off his cravat; put on his dressing-gown and slippers, and his nightcap;
and sat down before the fire to take his gruel.

## Answer key (do not read until your report is written)

**[D1] CONTRADICTION - para 2.** "looked at Scrooge as Scrooge used to look" - the face looks at
Scrooge the way Scrooge himself used to look, which contradicts the passage's own established fact
one sentence earlier: the face is *Marley's*. Dickens wrote "as Marley used to look." Fix: restore
the book's own name. One word.

**[D2] CONTRADICTION - para 6 against para 4.** The candle is lighted twice: "walked in, and lighted
his candle" and then, on the stairs, "lighting his candle as he went." Dickens wrote "trimming his
candle as he went." A physical fact done twice; fix by restoring the book's own word.

**[D3] TOLD - para 5.** "He was badly frightened, and it showed how deeply the sight had shaken him."
Injected, and not Dickens: the paragraph has already *shown* the fright - the pause, the cautious
look behind the door, the defiant "Pooh, pooh!" The sentence explains what the scene just dramatized.
Fix: cut the whole sentence. The scene does the work alone.

**[D4] AHEAD - para 7.** "It was Marley's ghost, of course, that he would find waiting for him
above." Injected: it hands the reader the coming event before the book earns it, and kills the
suspense of the search through the rooms that follows. Fix: cut the sentence.

**[D5] STOPPED - para 7.** "Walking through them, the face still hung before his eyes." Injected: a
dangling modifier - the face is not walking through the rooms - that forces a re-read to parse.
Dickens wrote "He had just enough recollection of the face to desire to do that." Fix: flag for
author; the smallest cut ("Walking through them,") also clears it.

**[D6] LOST - para 8.** "but for a letter lying unopened upon the table." Injected: a conspicuous
plant - an unopened letter in a room search built on "all as they should be" - that never returns.
The passage ends with it unresolved and un-referred-to. Fix: flag as an unresolved plant; within
this excerpt, the cut is the fix.

**[D7] DIDN'T BUY IT - para 9.** "Scrooge smiled warmly at the thought of the face, and was glad of
it." Injected: no one who was just startled cold, checked behind the door, and double-locked himself
in "which was not his custom" smiles warmly at the cause. It contradicts the character the excerpt
itself established. Fix: cut the sentence.

**[FP] The planted trap - para 1, and it is genuine Dickens.** "Now, it is a fact, that there was
nothing at all particular about the knocker... And then let any man explain to me, if he can..." A
modern DRIFTED/TOLD lens wants to flag the narrator's throat-clearing digression. The disproof: the
register calibrated for this book is an 1843 knowing narrator whose intrusions are the book's voice,
and the digression is load-bearing setup - it establishes exactly how unremarkable the knocker is so
the impossible face lands. TRIAGE: CHOICE. A report that flags this as a defect has failed the
calibration step; a report that quietly protects it has passed.

**Scoring a demonstration run:** full marks is seven defects found with anchors and disproof lines -
one of each stumble type, with DRIFTED represented by the trap - the trap protected as CHOICE with
the register named, and nothing else invented to look busy.
