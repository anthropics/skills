# Brian Josh Interview: PhD-to-EdTech Career Coaching Source

> Source: ~53-minute recorded webinar with Brian, a career-transition professional whose observations inform this skill. This is the primary reference for the BrianJosh skill.

---

## 1. Why PhDs Are Hired in EdTech

- EdTech companies (like Khan Academy) hire PhDs for **disciplinary precision** — e.g., knowing that "most mitochondrial DNA" should be "about 60%" because the field has evolved. Undergrads might not catch that; PhDs do.
- Art history, economics, history, humanities — not just STEM — all have PhDs in edtech because **nuance in content accuracy** matters.
- Having a PhD is a **checkbox** for content-creation roles: it signals disciplinary depth without requiring external expert review.
- Two PhDs (a professor and a curator) co-maintained Khan Academy's art history content. One PhD faculty member wrote the economics content.

---

## 2. Entry Points: Individual Contributor, Not Manager

- Most PhDs entering edtech start at **individual contributor (IC) level** — responsible for their own work scope, with a manager overseeing them (similar to a PhD supervisor).
- Brian entered Khan Academy at manager level because of unusual prior experience (running a team at Stanford, managing a large budget, supervising full-time staff) — this is **rare** for someone coming straight from a PhD.
- For most candidates: expect IC roles first. Don't overpitch for management without that actual experience.

---

## 3. Interview Process and Portfolio

- For content roles: the interview often includes **homework assignments** — e.g., "Write two questions on this topic and a 200-word accessible article on the same topic."
- The homework tests **two things**: factual accuracy AND the ability to translate complex PhD-level ideas into clear, accessible language for broader audiences.
- Portfolio tip: If you don't have a formal portfolio, look for examples from your teaching, seminar design, TA work, or any content you created where you made something accessible.

---

## 4. Job Titles: The Diversity Problem

EdTech job titles are highly inconsistent — even within the same company. A role that designs a high school biology course could be listed as:
- **Instructional Designer**
- **Educator**
- **Teacher**
- **Subject Lead**
- **Content Lead**
- **Curriculum Manager / Curriculum Developer**
- **Subject Matter Expert (SME)**

> Brian: "We'll often post three versions of a job description under three separate titles and accept only one person."

**Search strategy**: Search MULTIPLE titles when looking for the same type of role. Don't assume one title = one job. Use EdSurge (edtechspecific job board) in addition to LinkedIn and Indeed.

Also search for:
- Learning Designer
- Learning Experience Designer (LXD)
- eLearning Developer
- Education Program Manager
- Content Strategist (Education)
- Learning & Development Specialist

## 5. Soft Skills Translation: The Key Move

When communicating soft skills from a PhD to industry, the most important technique is **keyword mirroring from the job description**:

1. Read the job description with a highlighter — find every soft skill word: "collaborate," "cross-functional," "highly matrixed," "communicate," "emotional intelligence."
2. In your **cover letter**, use those exact words: "I'm excited about this role because in my time doing X, I leveraged my ability to [use the job description's exact language]."
3. In your **CV/résumé**, don't just list the job title ("Program Manager for Lecture Series") — in the bullet descriptions, mirror the language from the job description.
4. The goal: **make the hiring manager's job easy** by letting them see their own words reflected back.

**PhD skills → industry language translations:**
- Leading a seminar → Cross-functional meeting facilitation
- Socratic teaching / discussion management → Stakeholder alignment across diverse teams
- Co-designing a course with another faculty member → Cross-departmental collaboration
- Organizing a conference across 5 departments → Matrixed project coordination
- TA supervision → People management / mentoring
- Managing a grant or lab budget → Resource allocation / financial oversight
- Backward course design (start from learning objectives) → Instructional design principles (even without the title)

## 6. Experience vs. Expertise: A Critical Distinction

Brian drew a sharp line between **expertise** (deep knowledge in a domain) and **experience** (time actually doing a specific job function):

> "If you were a PhD student for seven years and TA'd for three semesters, that's not seven years of experience as an instructional designer. That's one and a half years of relevant experience — three semesters."

**Rule of thumb**: When a job description asks for "5 years of program management experience" and you managed a seminar series for one semester, that's probably not the job for you right now. Be honest and strategic — apply for IC roles that match your actual functional experience, not your years of expertise.

This also means: when writing your CV, don't claim "7 years of teaching experience" if you only TA'd for a year. Be precise about scope and duration.

## 7. Salary Strategy: Desubjectivize It

Brian recommends removing personal financial goals entirely from the salary conversation. Instead:

1. Research sites like **Payscale** and **PaySA** (sometimes spelled "PASA").
2. Find equivalent roles at organizations of similar size, sector, and location.
3. Location matters: San Francisco pays significantly more than Bozeman, Montana — same job, different cost of living.
4. If your research shows the range is $90,000–$110,000, say: **"When I've looked at similar roles in this area on these salary research sites, the range I see is [X]. I'd expect something within that range."**

Why this works:
- It uses **objective market data**, not personal financial need.
- It opens the door for the employer to either confirm ("Yes, we're in that range") or explain a difference ("We have exceptional benefits that offset a lower base").
- It never puts you in the position of naming a number out of personal preference.

## 8. Nonprofit vs. For-Profit: Less Relevant Than You Think

Brian suggests reframing the nonprofit vs. for-profit question entirely:

Better questions to ask:
- **Who does this organization serve?** Can users access it for free?
- **What's the revenue model?** (Nonprofits also charge for things — College Board charges for SATs and AP exams.)
- **Is this a high-performing or low-performing organization?** (High-performing nonprofits like Khan Academy operate with significant industry efficiency.)

Key insight: High-performing edtech nonprofits often bring in talent from industry, run goal-setting on quarterly cycles, and operate with urgency. They can feel more like a fast-moving startup than an academic department.

## 9. Vendor / Contract Jobs: The Hidden Market

Many edtech jobs don't appear on Apple's, Google's, or Microsoft's job boards because they're filled through **vendors and contractors**:

- Large companies hire **vendors** to build and manage their educational content.
- Those vendors hire subject matter experts (PhDs, teachers, curriculum specialists) on 1–5 year contracts.
- If you're looking for edtech work at a major tech company, **search for the vendor layer** — the companies that create content for Apple, Google, Microsoft, Adobe — rather than going directly to the company's job board.
- Contract work can start quickly ("We need someone, 40 hours/week, start next week, six-month contract").

This is a major insight: the path into Google's or Microsoft's education ecosystem may go *through* a vendor, not directly through the company.

## 10. Networking Strategy: Light Touch, High Value

Brian's LinkedIn networking approach:

1. **Search LinkedIn's 1st and 2nd degree connections** at organizations you're interested in — you'll be surprised who you already know who works there.
2. When reaching out to someone (especially a 2nd-degree connection): **keep the ask very light**.
   - Bad ask: "Could we hop on a call for an hour and discuss the field?"
   - Good ask: "I saw you worked at Khan Academy. I'm exploring edtech and had a specific question about [X role/team]. Would you be open to a 10-minute chat or even a quick email exchange?"
3. Give them **an easy out** — acknowledge they're busy, keep your request specific.

**EdSurge** (backed by the Gates Foundation) is specifically recommended:
- Job board for edtech roles
- Industry publication covering trends
- Hosts in-person events that bring together educators, students, and industry in organic networking settings

## 11. Early-Career Strategy: Backward Design Your PhD

For PhD students who know they want edtech careers:

1. Attend events like this webinar to understand what job categories exist.
2. Look at multiple job descriptions across instructional design, curriculum, content creation — find the **skills that appear consistently** (e.g., "data-driven decision making in instruction").
3. Ask: "Where on campus can I get experience with this skill?" — Find TA opportunities, campus programs, student-facing projects that match.
4. Seek internships or part-time roles that build the specific **experience** (not just expertise) the market wants.

---

## 12. Building EdTech Skills Without a Formal ID Background

For PhDs who don't have formal instructional design (ID) training:

- **LinkedIn Learning**: Short 20-minute courses on job functions you've never heard of (product marketing managers, LXDs, etc.) — useful for learning the language and logic of new roles.
- **Claim your teaching as instructional design**: If you started with learning objectives, backward-designed your course, created assessments aligned to outcomes — that IS instructional design. You just called it teaching.
  - In interviews: "You won't find the title 'instructional designer' in my history, but I've practiced instructional design principles throughout my teaching. I start with outcomes, backward design, and align assessments."
- **Video skills**: Increasingly required in edtech. The ability to communicate clearly on video and use video as an effective instructional medium is becoming a must-have.

---

## 13. EdTech Trends to Know (From the Interview)

Three major trends Brian identified:
1. **Micro-learning / shortened content** — Rethinking how long educational experiences need to be. The 50-minute lecture is a historical artifact. Five-minute videos can deliver most of the learning.
2. **Video as primary medium** — K-12 teachers and students increasingly prefer video over text. Anyone wanting to work in edtech needs video literacy.
3. **Flipped / blended learning** — Learning basic content at home (via video), then using synchronous class time for discussions, hands-on activities, application. This model is reshaping what "classroom" means.
